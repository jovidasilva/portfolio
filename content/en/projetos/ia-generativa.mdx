I wanted to understand how a language model works on the inside, and the way I found was to build one. No pre-trained model and no paid API. The tokenizer, the Transformer, the training and the chat interface are all part of the project itself.

## How I built it

I started with the tokenizer, a BPE with 16,000 tokens. The first version recounted every pair on each step and was far too slow, so I rewrote the counting to be incremental.

The model is a decoder-only Transformer in PyTorch, with 8 layers and about 25 million parameters. Pre-training used 1.5 billion characters of Portuguese Wikipedia and took around 6 hours on an RTX 4060 Ti. Then came fine-tuning on the Canarim dataset, about 143,000 question and answer pairs, plus dialogues I generated with a script.

The card's 8 GB of memory was the limit the whole time. With bfloat16 and gradient accumulation, I managed to test a version with up to 126 million parameters.

The chat runs on Flask. Math, conversions, dates and random draws are handled by regular code, and the interface shows when a tool gave the answer. There's also web search, which shows its sources. Training and chat live in separate Docker containers, with pytest tests and CI on GitHub Actions.

## Results

To compare versions, I put together a fixed test of 85 questions. The best version gets 69% of them right. It does well on what it saw in training and on questions reworded in other ways, but struggles when it needs to remember something said earlier in the conversation: there, accuracy drops to 47%.

## What I learned

A model this size imitates what a calculation looks like, but it can't actually calculate. So math stays with the calculator, and the model only handles the conversation.
