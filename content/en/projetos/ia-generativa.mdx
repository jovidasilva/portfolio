I wanted to understand how a language model works on the inside, and the way I found was to build one. No pre-trained model and no paid API. The tokenizer, the Transformer, the training and the chat interface are all part of the project itself. The result is NADIA, which stands for Núcleo Autônomo de Diálogo Inteligente e Adaptável (Autonomous Core for Intelligent, Adaptive Dialogue).

## How I built it

I started with the tokenizer, a BPE with 16,000 tokens. The first version recounted every pair on each step and was far too slow, so I rewrote the counting to be incremental.

The model is a decoder-only Transformer in PyTorch. The first version had 8 layers and about 25 million parameters; NADIA has 16 layers and 126 million. Pre-training used 1.5 billion characters of Portuguese Wikipedia, on an RTX 4060 Ti. Then came fine-tuning on the Canarim dataset, about 143,000 question and answer pairs, plus dialogues I generated with a script.

The card's 8 GB of memory was the limit the whole time. Bfloat16 and gradient accumulation are what made the 126-million version possible.

The chat runs on Flask. Math, conversions, dates and random draws are handled by regular code, and the interface shows when a tool gave the answer. There's also web search, which shows its sources. Training and chat live in separate Docker containers, with pytest tests and CI on GitHub Actions.

For the site, I exported the model to ONNX with 8-bit weights, which shrank the file from 510 to 140 MB, and rewrote the tokenizer and generation in TypeScript. That way NADIA runs in the visitor's browser, with no server, and the test results stayed practically the same as the original model's.

## Results

To compare versions, I built a fixed test of 85 questions and another of 40 questions that have no possible answer, about made-up people and places. With no filter, NADIA gets 66% of the 85 right, but invents an answer for almost all of the 40, in the same confident tone as the correct ones.

The fix was to use the probability the model itself gives to each word it writes. Below 70% average confidence, NADIA says it doesn't know. With this filter, invented answers dropped from 37 to 5 out of 40, and precision rose from 46% to 68%, at the cost of answering fewer questions.

Two experiments didn't work, and went into the paper anyway. Going from 25 to 126 million parameters made the text much more fluent but barely changed accuracy: for its size, the bigger model saw too little data. And teaching "I don't know" with made-up names made things worse: the model memorized the shape of the questions and started denying real things, like the existence of Lisbon.

## What I learned

A model this size imitates what a calculation looks like, but it can't actually calculate. So math stays with the calculator, and the model only handles the conversation.

It also can't tell when it doesn't know. But the probability it gives to its own words can, and that's what decides when NADIA answers.
